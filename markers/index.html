Lab markers, read with the assay in mind

What a marker measures, what moves it, and why the method printed on your report changes the answer. Reference pages for the bloodwork people on hormone therapy and peptide protocols actually run.

Last reviewed: 3 September 2026

The gap these pages fill

Search for a lab marker and you will find a range, a bolded "optimal" number and no mention of how the value was produced. That omission is the single most common way people misread their own bloodwork.

Sensitive (LC/MS-MS) estradiol and standard immunoassay estradiol are not the same measurement. Direct-immunoassay free testosterone, calculated free testosterone and equilibrium dialysis are three different answers to the same question. A ferritin drawn after a blood donation is telling you about the donation. TherapyLog stores the assay method as part of the result rather than throwing it away, and these pages are written the same way: whenever the method changes the interpretation, the page names it.

Three rules every page here follows

  • Your lab's printed range wins. The reference interval on your own report belongs to the assay and the instrument that produced your number. Where a page shows a generic range, it is labelled generic — it is a fallback, not a correction of your report.
  • Optimal bands are non-diagnostic. Where a page shows an optimal band drawn from clinical literature and community practice, it is there for reading a trend over time. It does not diagnose anything, and being outside one is not a finding.
  • Interpretation ends with your clinician. These pages describe what the literature and community practice report, and show the basis for every number. They do not tell a reader with a particular result to take a particular drug at a particular dose. Take the result to the person who prescribes for you.

The pages being written

Fifteen markers first, chosen because they are the ones this audience tests and the ones where the existing results are weakest. Each gets the assay variants, the units and their conversions, the generic range, the non-diagnostic optimal band where one exists, and authored explanation with primary sources. Nothing is linked below until the page is live, and the sequence below is the working list, not a publication schedule.

  • Estradiol — sensitive versus standard assay, including what elevated estradiol on testosterone therapy is actually reported to feel like, with the assay named on every number quoted.
  • Total testosterone — immunoassay versus LC/MS-MS, and how the reference range shifts by age.
  • Free versus total testosterone — calculated, direct and equilibrium dialysis, and when the three disagree.
  • SHBG — what raises and lowers it, and why it drives the free testosterone number.
  • Hematocrit on testosterone therapy, with hemoglobin, red cell count and the ferritin question that comes with donation.
  • Prolactin — what actually raises it, and what a single elevated draw does and does not mean.
  • LH and FSH — what they show during and after suppression.
  • IGF-1 — why the range is age-banded, and what growth hormone secretagogues do to it.
  • HbA1c and fasting glucose, including the mmol/mol to percent conversion and what changes on GLP-1 medication.
  • ApoB versus LDL cholesterol — direct, calculated and particle count.
  • Lipoprotein(a) — and why mg/dL and nmol/L cannot be converted into one another.
  • Ferritin and the iron panel — iron, TIBC, transferrin and saturation read together.
  • Vitamin D — nmol/L to ng/mL, and what the ranges are based on.
  • The thyroid panel — TSH, free T4, free T3, reverse T3 and antibodies.
  • DHT — what shifts it, and what 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors do to it.
